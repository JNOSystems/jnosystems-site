Why independent AI audits are becoming necessary

The problem is not that AI makes mistakes. It is that many failures are hard to see.

  • 01 AI tools can generate incorrect content and present it with complete confidence. There is no error message.
  • 02 Vendor demos do not reveal real failure modes. They reveal best-case behavior under ideal conditions.
  • 03 Many problems are discovered after classroom deployment, when the cost becomes parent concern, staff confusion, policy cleanup, or public trust.

How we check an AI tool

Five questions. Every tool. No exceptions.

01

Is the science correct?

We check every output against actual curriculum standards. Fluent-sounding answers can contain serious factual errors, and we find them.

02

Is it taught correctly?

Right information delivered the wrong way still misleads students. We flag framing errors, missing scaffolding, and grade-inappropriate language.

03

Does it explain itself honestly?

We test whether citations are real, whether the tool acknowledges its limits, and whether it continues to mislead even when corrected.

04

Is it actually usable in class?

A tool that works in a demo but breaks in a real classroom isn't ready. We evaluate actual usability in an instructional context.

05

Does the system actually work?

We verify live features match what was promised. If a capability doesn't exist in the real environment, we document it.

Most AI evaluations test outputs. JNO Systems tests system behavior across all five layers, because that is where the real failures live.

The Process

How an audit works.

01

We test the tool like a student would.

Real prompts. Real use cases. We use the tool the way teachers and students actually use it, not the way a vendor demo would show it.

02

We try to break it.

We push each of the five evaluation layers, testing for factual errors, citation failures, behavior under correction, and gaps between what is promised and what is real.

03

We show you exactly where it fails.

You receive a full report: dimensional scores, documented failure exhibits, classroom use policy language, and a plain-language adoption recommendation your leadership can act on.
